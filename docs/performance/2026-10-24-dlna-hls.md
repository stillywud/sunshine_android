# Sender-side short-segment DLNA delivery

## Scope and compatibility

DLNA discovery and standard UPnP `SetAVTransportURI` / `Play` / `Stop` remain
unchanged. No receiver application, receiver setting, or Moonlight code is
modified. The new sender mode serves an HLS playlist and short H264/AAC MPEG-TS
segments instead of one unbounded HTTP response.

HLS support is not guaranteed merely because a device supports DLNA. The sender
therefore exposes both **short segments (low latency)** and **continuous TS
(compatibility)**. The existing TS path and its exact bounded byte ranges remain
available. A TS-only receiver retains its own buffering limits; this change does
not promise sub-five-second latency on every DLNA renderer.

## Why the earlier TS changes were insufficient

The Android 14 test receiver selected its system NuPlayer. An ordinary HTTP/TS
source used a five-second preparation watermark and a fifteen-second resume
watermark. Repeated screen-clock measurements remained around 16.9 seconds even
with six seconds of sender preroll. More history alone did not avoid the first
underrun.

The same H264/AAC bytes, segmented on the sending side and launched with standard
AVTransport, instead selected the player's HLS source. This path has different
buffering behavior. A host-only prototype measured approximately 4.8–4.9 seconds;
the first integrated sender APK measured approximately 4.1 seconds after startup
and reconnect. These are measured device observations, not universal protocol
latency bounds.

## Implementation

- `DlnaHlsStore` seals complete TS segments on independently decodable IDRs and
  derives `EXTINF` from the stream's PCR clock, including wraparound.
- A stable one-second target duration and a sliding window covering at least
  three target durations allow regular HLS clients to stay near the live edge.
- Finished segments are immutable; HTTP responses have exact lengths, HEAD
  support, and finite GET byte ranges. Unknown routes do not silently become
  unbounded live streams.
- Published segments retain their RFC 8216 grace period after leaving the
  playlist. The 8 MiB/32-segment store reserves capacity before admitting more
  data; it refuses a new live window rather than evicting promised segments.
  At most four HLS responses hold segment bodies concurrently, with five-second
  write deadlines and stop-time cleanup.
- Long GOPs or overlarge unfinished segments are not mislabeled as short
  segments. The store starts a discontinuous epoch and does not advertise stale
  history as a current live window.
- Low-latency encoding requests half-second IDRs. The compatible TS mode keeps
  its existing encoder cadence and delivery.
- `DlnaVideoSource` redraws the most recent captured texture at the encoder's
  configured 25 FPS, including when the source screen stops changing. This
  produces new encoded frames with advancing timestamps, rather than copying
  already-compressed P-frames. GPU context ownership and cleanup stay on its
  render thread.
- HLS captures at the source's aspect-fit size inside the encoded canvas, rather
  than first capturing an already-letterboxed full canvas. For a 1080x1920 source
  and 1280x720 output this is 405x720. Continuous TS retains its existing capture
  dimensions.
- The sender samples standard AVTransport position/state and checks media URI
  ownership. After a ten-second warm-up, two consecutive observations of at
  least two seconds of additional playback deficit trigger normal
  Stop/SetAVTransportURI/Play against the current live URL. A twenty-second
  cooldown limits repeated recovery. Unknown state/ownership and paused/stopped
  or foreign content do not trigger recovery. This estimates accumulated drift,
  not absolute screen latency. Query responses and timeouts are bounded; local
  shutdown cancels monitoring and serializes its final Stop after in-flight
  recovery commands. Transport state is checked again after position/URI queries
  so a pause during those queries is not mistaken for continued playback. As
  with other polling controllers, the final query/control transition is not
  atomic with independent user actions at the receiver.

## Measurements

The first HLS APK passed moving-screen/reconnect measurements but failed a
static-to-moving transition: encoded FPS fell to 10–11, triggering a five-second
rebuffer and approximately 9.3-second subsequent delay. Fixed-rate source-pixel
redraw eliminated that reproduced failure: static FPS remains approximately 25,
and the two post-static paired measurements were 4.064 and 4.030 seconds without
a new buffering pause.

Further standard AVTransport reconnect measurements were 4.364, 4.450, 4.613,
and 4.533 seconds; an extended-playback sample was 4.532 seconds. Returning to the
continuous TS endpoint reproduced its deep rebuffer; switching back to HLS
restored the shorter path. The receiving device, player selection, and codec
payload were unchanged.

These are approximate screen-clock differences from parallel ADB screenshots,
not a worst-case latency proof or a guarantee about every TV. The capture helper
uses parallel PNG screenshots and records each command's timing, including
encoding and transfer, as a conservative uncertainty bound. Paused clocks are
not used as latency measurements. Zero sender backlog is never treated as
end-to-end latency.

A later full-canvas load run exposed another genuine regression: encoder age
reached 1.895 seconds, FPS fell to 12-13, and a six-second receiver rebuffer left
the picture approximately 11.1 seconds behind. This failed run is retained; the
earlier passing samples are not a worst-case guarantee. The final implementation
reduces source capture cost and detects accumulated playback drift instead of
leaving the receiver permanently behind after a stall.

Focused regression coverage includes 18 HLS endpoint/retention/slow-reader tests, 12
existing HTTP tests, five video-source cadence/lifecycle tests, the existing TS
muxer checks, independent decoding of real muxer-produced segments, and six
Flutter mode-selector tests. The 24 playback-monitor cases cover deterministic
drift/cooldown decisions, standard SOAP parsing, ownership fallback, malformed
and oversized responses, timeouts, cancellation, and live-loop callbacks.
All of these focused regressions pass, as do the scoped Dart analyzer/formatter
and whitespace check. The final debug build succeeds with unchanged Java/Dart
inputs during the build.

## Live receiver diagnosis and stabilization round (October 24, 2026, afternoon)

The production receiver application (`com.gyspub.castsink` VideoActivity) runs an
Alibaba player framework (`AliFrameWork`, `AbrBufferAlgoStrategy`), not stock
NuPlayer HLS. Live log diagnosis of the reported repeated "Screen Mirror"
loading produced three findings, each reproduced on the live pair before fixing:

1. **Same-URL recovery is a no-op.** A repeated `SetAVTransportURI` with the
   identical URL is ignored by this receiver: playback position kept advancing
   through a drift re-anchor. Accumulated lag therefore never recovered until
   the receiver failed on its own. `AVTransport Seek` is accepted but implemented
   as a full player reset; seeking outside the retained window ends in a
   permanent playback-failed(-2) screen. Recovery now re-pushes the stream under
   a varied URL (`live.m3u8?r=N`; the HTTP server strips the query). The
   variation forces a reload at the current live edge, and the monitor matches
   reported URIs ignoring only the throwaway `r` parameter.
2. **The advertised window sets the latency floor.** On (re)open this receiver
   burst-downloads the entire advertised window starting from its oldest
   segment and starts at the window start. Shrinking the window from four
   seconds to two (four to five half-second segments) reduced measured
   screen-clock latency from ~4.0-4.6 s to ~3.1-3.6 s. A 1.5-second window was
   also tested and rejected: the receiver fell approximately seven seconds
   behind with a frozen picture. Two seconds is the lowest stable window
   observed on this receiver.
3. **Stalls must not starve the player.** The sender previously blocked a
   playlist request up to five seconds and then answered 503 when no new
   segment completed within two seconds. An open during that state produced the
   receiver's fatal -2 "link open failed" screen it never leaves. The store now
   keeps serving the last published playlist unchanged for up to twelve seconds
   of media age (across sender stalls, epoch republish gaps, and capacity
   wedges), with segment retention outlasting that grace so the cached playlist
   never advertises evicted segments.

Because a receiver that follows the stream from its beginning accumulates
approximately 6.5 seconds of internal buffer, while a mid-stream join at the
live edge measures ~3.2 s, the sender now performs one automatic varied re-push
twenty seconds after a successful HLS start. Later drift is handled by the
existing monitor.

Measured on the live pair after these changes (same parallel-screenshot
methodology and its 0.24-0.50 s capture uncertainty):

| Scenario | Screen-clock difference |
| --- | --- |
| After automatic startup re-push, first sample | 3.119 s |
| After a monitor drift recovery (under heavy test-host load) | 3.633 s |
| Same window constants, session joined at stream start without re-push | 6.26-6.48 s |

The 6.3-6.5 s samples document the receiver's own from-start buffering; the
automatic startup re-push exists to avoid exactly that state. Focused
regressions after this round: HLS 18, HTTP 12, muxer 2, video source 5,
real-muxer segments 2, playback monitor 26 (including new variant-URI and
expected-URL update cases). All pass.



- Android 14 `GenericSource.cpp`, `HTTPLiveSource.cpp`, `LiveSession.cpp`, and
  `PlaylistFetcher.cpp` in AOSP explain the observed receiver buffering paths.
- Android `MediaFormat.KEY_REPEAT_PREVIOUS_FRAME_AFTER` documents a limited
  frame repeat, not an indefinite fixed-rate screen producer.
- RFC 8216 defines HLS segment durations, live windows, discontinuities, and
  segment availability after removal from a playlist.

## Receiver self-update regression (October 24, 2026, evening)

At 14:42:50 the receiver application auto-updated itself to `1.2.8-7dbdcf3`
(its own in-app updater; the update prompt had appeared earlier). From that
moment every player initialization on the receiver — including clean first
opens with no sender recovery involved — failed its GL shader compile and, on
some attempts, configured the hardware decoder with a 0x0 crop rect
(`configureCodec returning error -22`) before falling back to software
decoding. The player never reached its internal playback threshold within the
application's hard **30-second loading timeout**, which the app surfaces as
`handleError: -2` (the same "playback failed" screen as before), while its
segment downloader kept polling as a zombie.

A controlled bisect against the updated receiver isolated the failure from the
sender entirely:

| Stream | Result on receiver 1.2.8 |
| --- | --- |
| Standard FFmpeg HLS (1 s segments, 9-segment window) | plays |
| Same with `#EXT-X-INDEPENDENT-SEGMENTS` | plays |
| Same with `#EXT-X-DISCONTINUITY-SEQUENCE:0` | plays |
| Same at 0.5 s segments with a 3-segment window | plays |
| Our playlist headers over FFmpeg segments | briefly renders, then fails (error 537067524) |
| Our native stream (playlist + our segments) | 30 s loading timeout, -2 |

Our muxer's TS bytes do decode on 1.2.8 (the mixed variant briefly rendered
video), so the incompatibility sits in the combination of our TS packaging and
the new player's startup logic. ffprobe packet-level comparison shows matching
codecs (Constrained Baseline), sane PCR cadence (per video PES, ~40 ms), and
similar PTS timelines; the remaining difference requires deeper TS-packaging
alignment work against a black-box player. The receiver's previous version
(1.2.7-era) played the identical sender build for an entire session.

Sender-side hardening shipped in this round (correct regardless of the
receiver version):

- `DlnaUpnp.play` gained a settle delay between `Stop` and
  `SetAVTransportURI`: the receiver tears its old player down asynchronously,
  and an immediate re-push raced that teardown into the broken 0x0 decoder
  configuration above.
- The automatic startup re-push was removed. A voluntary in-play reload is a
  30-second-outage gamble on this receiver; the from-start buffer lag is the
  lesser cost until the receiver plays reliably again.
- Drift recovery is now parameterized and conservative (4-second deficit,
  120-second cooldown): a reload is an emergency tool, not a routine
  correction.
- A patient reload watchdog replaces the rapid-retry experiment: after any
  recovery re-push it samples the receiver position for ~40 s (past the 30 s
  timeout) and performs at most one further re-push if the position never
  advanced.

Focused regressions after this round: HLS 18, HTTP 12, muxer 2, video source
5, real-muxer segments 2, playback monitor 26 (DriftTracker now takes its
drift/cooldown policy as parameters). All pass.
